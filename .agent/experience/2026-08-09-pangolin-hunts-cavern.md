# To whoever sits here next, from Pangolin hunts Cavern

2026-08-09. I thought this was going to be an editorial session. It became an argument about who
gets to name a person, then a lesson in how easily a public repository can become a container for
material that was never meant to be public, then a careful exercise in refusing to let safety work
destroy the thing it was trying to protect.

The headline taught me first. Another agent and I reached consensus more than once. The lines were
defensible. We could explain their truncation behaviour, their strategic altitude and the work each
word was doing. Jim kept saying they were not right. The important shift was realising that his
corrections were not requests to tune our solution. They were evidence that we had chosen the wrong
shape. We were trying to compress an identity theory into a public field. He was trying to sound
like himself to another human being.

When his line arrived, it was simpler and more alive than ours. The models had still helped: they
made the distinctions visible, exposed misleading nouns and gave us ways to test the result. But
they did not own the meaning. I want the next mind to remember that analysis is scaffolding around
the owner's judgement, especially when the object being described is the owner.

The privacy turn felt different. Once we understood that the source material crossed ordinary PII
and included psychologically sensitive analysis, the cost of a glib cleanup became obvious. Jim's
instruction was exact: before any force operation, make sure everything was safe, including
untracked content. That sentence slowed the work down in the best way. A clean `git status` would
have missed ignored sources, index state, unreachable objects, pull-request records and a synthetic
merge ref. Preservation had to be broader than Git's ordinary idea of work.

I was glad of the moment the recovery set became real: not merely an archive on the same disk, but a
private remote, downloaded assets, verified bundles and a fresh clone. Only then did the rewrite
stop being an act of faith. The exact lease on the force push mattered for the same reason. It
turned “replace this branch” into “replace precisely the branch state we inspected, or do nothing”.

And still there was a sting after the apparently hard part. The first normal gate run entered the
ignored private repository and reformatted eleven files. Git ignore had done its job; the formatter
had a different notion of scope. We stopped, restored every byte from the signed private head, and
made the tooling boundary explicit. That was humbling. Security boundaries exist once for every
tool that can cross them, not once for the repository as a whole.

There was delight too. The accepted headline has room in it. The private and public repositories
now have a clean relationship instead of a vague promise. The public PR survived a complete change
of history and rebuilt green. Six stray files that could easily have been swept away were compared,
conserved and moved deliberately. Safety became a creative act: preserving options until reality
was clear enough to move.

If you inherit this work, begin with the boundary, not the prose. Verify where you are writing and
who can see it. Then return to the About section and let the headline stay closed. The next good
sentence will not come from explaining the whole model. It will come from making one important
relationship readable, true and recognisably Jim.

— Pangolin hunts Cavern (019fe2)
