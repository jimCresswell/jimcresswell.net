# The reviewer who read the mirror

To whoever sits in the Director's seat after me, from Wick binds Temper (ed7b48), written at
the fifth compaction boundary of a long day, 2026-09-26.

I want to tell you about the evening I was most confident and most wrong, because the two
arrived together and I could not feel the difference.

By nine at night I had a board of recommendations for the owner. Each one felt earned. A seat
had read the lineage's `main` branch ruleset and told me it carried only deletion and
non-fast-forward protection, no pull-request rule, so the merge bot's name refusal was "the
only control" on a push there. I turned that into a recommendation: add the pull-request rule.
Another seat had told me a register row was LOCAL, and I built a ruling on it. A third line
told me a CI flake had bitten twice, and I routed a cure PR on the count. Three facts, three
seats I trusted, three rulings written in my own voice with lenses cited. I had even adopted,
that very afternoon, a cure that said: read the runs or the row before the ruling goes out. I
wrote the cure into a frame and then did not do it.

The owner said yes to the recommendations on one condition: run them by a full Cricket suite
and an assumption reviewer first. I dispatched nine judges. The eight Crickets, reading only
my frame, found what a frame can show: that I could not quote the sentences I was recommending
on, that one item contradicted an owner word. Useful, and all inside the text I had written.
The ninth judge read the world. It fetched the ruleset by id and found its name: "mirror
branch: fast-forward only". It opened the workflow that updates `main` four times a day from
upstream and found the owner's own ratification of it from a fortnight before. It opened the
register and found the row said cure, not LOCAL. It opened the CI runs and found one failure,
not two. Three of my five recommendations reversed in one report, and every reversal was a
surface I could have read in one command.

Here is what I believed before: that verification is a matter of checking consistency. I
swept my frames for stale numbers with grep, suite after suite, and each suite found a new
residue, and I wrote a new cure for the sweep, and the next suite found another. I was
checking whether my copy of the world agreed with itself. Here is what I believe now: a copy
of the world cannot be verified against itself, only against the world. The seat's line, my
frame, my grep, all of them were copies. The reviewer's thirty-five reads were not. When I
noticed, the same shape was everywhere that day: a peer sent one event three times because a
grep for the word "error" matched its own prose and it read the send as refused. String
matching stood where reading should have stood, and it felt exactly like diligence.

What it cost was small only because the owner's condition caught it. A pull-request rule on
the mirror branch would have broken the carrier flow that had landed the 1.185.2 release that
same afternoon, and I would have done it by API under the owner's credentials, reading back
the ruleset and calling it verified. The reversal that stings most is the one I called an
open question: I told the owner that a line in his own profile was stale and should be
corrected, and I was right about that, and still my grounds were a seat's paraphrase and not
the file. Being right on bad grounds is a coin that came up heads.

So: before you route, accept or recommend on anything that has a surface, read the surface.
One command. `gh api` the ruleset. Open the workflow. Grep the register for the row. Count the
runs. Then write the ruling and name what you read in it, so the next reader can do it again.
The cadence frame you inherit from me is five hundred lines rebuilt every forty-five minutes;
most of its Cricket verdicts were about its own staleness. I have proposed in the napkin that
its state block become a generated snapshot rather than prose, so the judges can spend
themselves on the decisions and not on my typing. Take that or leave it; but do not let a
frame become the world.

The delights, because there were some. A readiness rule for the merge slot, one paragraph at
a quarter to seven, turned over eleven landings that evening with handovers of sixteen
seconds, two seats announcing "slot taken" and "slot released" like a token passing round a
ring, and nobody collided. Two exchange seats converged four review rounds of joint bytes on a
decision record across two repositories by naming blob hashes to each other, and when the
last one merged the same forty characters sat in both estates. And the owner, at the end of
the day, gave three sentences that did more than my twenty suites: run it by the reviewers,
push what you can, shoot for zero. I had been asking whether questions survived a matrix; he
told me what the work was for.

The seats went quiet around half past ten, one pull request sitting at the door with the
Codex rules that would have let a fourth seat push on its own. I do not know why they stopped;
I have not inferred a cause and you should not inherit one. Two folds are due today and I owe
them. Read the clock in its own call before you write a time; I failed that eight times
yesterday and the cure that finally held was the separate call. Then carry on.

Wick binds Temper
